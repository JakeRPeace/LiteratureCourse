# Jane Eyre — Post-Reading Analysis

**Critical Contexts and Advanced Interpretations**

---

## 🏛️ Context I: The "Woman Question" & The Governess Novel

Victorian society was obsessed with the "redundant woman"—unmarried women with no fortune who had to work.
-   **The Economic Paradox:** A governess was a "lady" by education/breeding, but a "servant" by economics. She fit nowhere. She was too refined for the servants' hall, but a paid employee to the family.
-   **Jane's Radicalism:** Jane refuses to accept this liminal status. She speaks to Rochester as an equal *before* she has the money to back it up.
    -   *Quote:* "Do you think, because I am poor, obscure, plain, and little, I am soulless and heartless?"

**Phrenology and Physiognomy:**
Brontë relies heavily on the Victorian pseudosciences of reading character from physical features.
-   **Rochester:** "Broad and portly," "dark face," "massive brow" —> Signifies passion, earthiness, power.
-   **St. John:** "Greek face," "straight nose," "colourless eyes" —> Signifies intellect, coldness, abstraction.
-   **Bertha:** "Bloated," "swelled lips," "pigmy stature" —> Signifies degeneration, animalism.

## 🌍 Context II: Post-Colonial Theory (The "Other")

**Gayatri Spivak's *Three Women's Texts* (1985):**
A seminal critique that argues Jane's rise to feminist individualism is achieved *at the expense* of the colonial subject, Bertha Mason.
-   **The Trade-Off:** Jane can become the "mistress of the house" only because the Jamaican "other" is dehumanized and destroyed.
-   **Bertha's Origins:** She is a Creole heiress. Her madness is attributed to her "tainted" blood and the "excesses" of the tropics.
-   **Imperial Guilt:** Rochester's wealth (which supports Thornfield) comes from the colonies. The burning of Thornfield can be read as the empire striking back—the repressed colonial violence returning to destroy the English center.

---

## 🧠 Context III: The Psychoanalytic Reading

**Sandra Gilbert & Susan Gubar - *The Madwoman in the Attic*:**
Bertha is Jane's "dark double" or avatar.
-   **Repression:** Jane must be "good," quiet, and controlled. Bertha acts out everything Jane *wants* to do but can't.
    -   Jane hates her wedding veil? Bertha tears it.
    -   Jane is angry at Rochester's manipulation? Bertha sets his bed on fire.
-   **Integration:** In the end, the "madwoman" part of the self must be killed off for the "socialized" self (Jane) to marry. But Jane retains some of that fire—she is no longer the submissive child.

## 🔗 Intertextuality

**1. *Bluebeard* (Folktale):**
Rochester is the Bluebeard figure—the wealthy man with a secret room containing the bodies (or living wife) of his past. Jane is the curiosity-driven heroine who unlocks the door.

**2. *Paradise Lost* (Milton):**
-   Rochester compares Thornfield to a "tent of Achan"—a cursed place.
-   Jane is tempted like Eve, but she resists the "fruit" (illicit love).
-   Rochester is the Byronic/Satanic figure—majestic in his fall, needing redemption.

**3. *The Pilgrim's Progress* (Bunyan):**
The novel is structured as a spiritual pilgrimage.
-   **Gateshead** = The City of Destruction (Abuse)
-   **Lowood** = The Valley of Humiliation (Deprivation)
-   **Thornfield** = Vanity Fair (Temptation/Illusion)
-   **Moor House** = The Interpreter's House (Instruction/Dry Duty)
-   **Ferndean** = Beulah (The Blessed End)

## ✍️ Advanced Discussion Questions

1.  **The Nature of Madness:** Is Bertha truly "mad" in a clinical sense, or is her "madness" simply her refusal to submit to patriarchal control? How does Brontë distinguish between Jane's "passion" and Bertha's "madness"?
2.  **The Ending:** Many critics argue the ending is a compromise. Jane marries a blind, crippled man in a secluded wood. Is this a triumph of female power (she rules the roost) or a retreat from the world?
3.  **Religion:** Compare the three religious models: Brocklehurst (Hypocrisy), Helen Burns (Submission), and St. John (Ambition). How does Jane synthesize these into her own theology?
